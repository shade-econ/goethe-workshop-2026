Cyclical income risk

## Slide 4: Cyclical income risk

- Recall canonical model: household takes $n_{it}=N_t$ as given and solves

$$
\max_{\{c_{it}\}} \mathbb{E}_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta_i^t\left(u(c_{it})-v(n_{it})\right)
$$

subject to

$$
c_{it}+a_{it}\le (1+r_t^p)a_{it-1}+(1-\tau_t)\frac{W_t}{P_t}n_{it}e_{it}
$$

$$
a_{it}\ge 0
$$

- This is restrictive: hours $n_{it}$ don’t move uniformly across people over the cycle!
- Relaxing this assumption will make a difference, but also uncover a big “puzzle”

## Slide 5: Introducing unequal rationing

- Simple way to relax is to assume that labor is rationed as a function of $e_{it}$, per

$$
n_{it}=N_t\frac{(e_{it})^{\zeta\log N_t}}{\mathbb{E}\left[e_i^{1+\zeta\log N_t}\right]}
\equiv N_t\Gamma(e_{it},N_t)
$$

assuming normalization $N_{ss}=1$.

- $\zeta>0$: procyclical inequality+income risk, $\zeta<0$ countercyclical, $\zeta=0$ acyclical

## Slide 6: Introducing unequal rationing

- Simple way to relax is to assume that labor is rationed as a function of $e_{it}$, per

$$
n_{it}=N_t\frac{(e_{it})^{\zeta\log N_t}}{\mathbb{E}\left[e_i^{1+\zeta\log N_t}\right]}
\equiv N_t\Gamma(e_{it},N_t)
$$

assuming normalization $N_{ss}=1$.

- $\zeta>0$: procyclical inequality+income risk, $\zeta<0$ countercyclical, $\zeta=0$ acyclical
- In particular the distribution of pretax income $y_{it}=\frac{W_t}{P_t}n_{it}e_{it}$ moves with the cycle

$$
\operatorname{sd}(\log y_{it})
=
(1+\zeta\log N_t)\operatorname{sd}(\log e_i)
$$

- Matters because:
  1. current shocks redistribute between different MPCs, and
  2. future shocks change perceived income risk

## Slide 7: How does this change the model?

- Now post-tax income $z_{it}$ for individual $i$ is

$$
z_{it}=(Y_t-T_t)\Gamma(e_{it},Y_t)
$$

- This results in a consumption function $\mathcal{C}_t(\{r_s,Y_s,T_s\})$. DAG:

Image note: The DAG has a box labeled “exogenous: $r$; unknown: $Y$.” This box sends $r$ to a “Fiscal” node, sends $r,Y$ directly to an “HH” node, and sends $Y$ to the “goods market.” The Fiscal node sends $T$ to HH. HH sends consumption $C$ to the goods market. The goods market is the final clearing node.

## Slide 8: Practical implementation with hetinput:

- Simple modification of the “hetinput” to the household block:

```python
def income_cyclical(Y,T, e_grid, e_pdf, zeta):
    y = (Y-T) * e_grid ** (1 + zeta * np.log(Y)) / np.vdot(e_grid ** (1 + zeta * np.log(Y)), e_pdf)
    return y

household_cyc = hh.add_hetinputs([income_cyclical])

print('Household block inputs:' , household_cyc.inputs)
print('Household block outputs:' , household_cyc.outputs)
```

Output:

```text
Household block inputs: ['a_grid', 'r', 'beta', 'eis', 'Pi', 'Y', 'T', 'e_grid', 'e_pdf', 'zeta']
Household block outputs: ['A', 'C']
```

## Slide 10: What is happening?

- Go back to zero liquidity limit $B_{ss}\to 0$. Now, can show:

$$
C_t=\delta\cdot C_{t+1}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\cdot \text{const}\cdot (r_t-\bar r)
$$

- There is dynamic discounting $\delta<1$ iff procyclical income risk $\zeta>0$
- Dynamic amplification $\delta>1$ iff countercyclical income risk $\zeta<0$
- Countercyclical income risk usually thought to be more plausible:
  - Theoretically: eg, from unemployment risk (Ravn-Sterk, Challe, Kekre…)
  - Empirically: eg, work by Guvenen&co and Storesletten&co
- Bilbiie’s “catch-22”: countercyclical risk plausible at micro level, not macro!

## Slide 11: Indirect ways to make income risk cyclical

- In richer models, income of agents typically involves multiple components,

$$
y_{it}=\frac{W_t}{P_t}n_{it}e_{it}-T_{it}+Tr_{it}
$$

where $T_{it}$ denotes taxes and $Tr_{it}$ denotes transfers.

- These also matter for cyclicality of income risk
- For example, suppose we set taxes to keep $(1+r_t)B_t$ constant as in our baseline, but that transfers are (nontraded) dividends from firms with sticky prices
- Both $T_{it}$ and $Tr_{it}$ fall when $r_t\downarrow$ (why?)
- Suppose that $T_{it}$ are paid by the high $e_{it}$, but everyone owns equal share of firms
- Then income risk is procyclical! This is McKay, Nakamura, Steinsson

## Slide 12: Maturity structure

Maturity structure

## Slide 13: Maturity structure

- So far: agents trade short term assets. What if longer maturities / duration?
- For tractability, assume “Calvo bonds”
  - buy one bond today for $q_t$, coupon payments $1,\delta,\delta^2,\ldots$
- New household problem:

$$
\max_{\{c_{it}\}} \mathbb{E}_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_i^t\left(u(c_{it})-v(N_t)\right)
$$

subject to

$$
c_{it}+q_t\lambda_{it}\le (1+\delta q_t)\lambda_{it-1}+Z_t e_{it}
$$

$$
\lambda_{it}\ge 0
$$

Here $\lambda_{it-1}$ is the total number of coupons owned coming into $t$.

- Asset pricing condition:

$$
1+r_t=\frac{1+\delta q_{t+1}}{q_t}
$$

## Slide 14: Rewriting the problem

- Redefine $a_{it}=q_t\lambda_{it}$. Using asset pricing condition, we have

$$
\max_{\{c_{it}\}} \mathbb{E}_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_i^t\left(u(c_{it})-v(N_t)\right)
$$

subject to

$$
c_{it}+a_{it}\le (1+r_t^p)a_{it-1}+Z_t e_{it}
$$

$$
a_{it}\ge 0
$$

and

$$
r_{t+1}^p=r_t,\quad t\ge 0
$$

$$
1+r_0^p=\frac{1+\delta q_0}{q_{ss}}
$$

- Lower ex-ante $r_t\to$ higher bond price $q_0\to$ higher $r_0^p$ (capital gain)
- Same for government budget constraint:

$$
B_t=(1+r_t^p)B_{t-1}+G_t-T_t
$$

- here $B_t\equiv q_t\lambda_t$ is market value of gov debt. Assume fiscal rule $\lambda_t=\lambda_{ss}$, $G_t=G_{ss}$

## Slide 16: Effect of maturity structure explained

- $\delta\uparrow$: households make cap. gains from monetary expansion, gov makes loss
- Redistribution from taxpayers to bond holders. Latter have lower MPCs, so aggregate effect on spending falls!
- Expect monetary policy to be more powerful in countries with short durations (eg ARMs)
- Actually true in data! (Calza et al)

Red dots for long bonds decline as $\delta$ rises, while a dashed short-bonds line is flat around 2.20. The chart visually shows that greater duration reduces the impact output response. ## Slide 18: Nominal assets

- So far, all assets are real. But in practice, many assets are nominal
  - Mortgages, bonds, etc
  - Creates very large exposures to inflation risk via nominal positions (see e.g. Doepke and Schneider)
- Consider 1-period bonds:

$$
\max_{\{c_{it}\}} \mathbb{E}_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_i^t\left(u(c_{it})-v(N_t)\right)
$$

subject to

$$
P_t c_{it}+A_{it}\le (1+i_t)A_{it-1}+P_t Z_t e_{it}
$$

$$
A_{it}\ge 0
$$

Can relax to have $P_t\underline{a}$.

In practice, borrowing constraints interact with inflation in complex ways! (“Tilt effect”)

- Fisher equation:

$$
1+r_t=\frac{(1+i_t)P_t}{P_{t+1}}
$$

## Slide 19: Rewriting the problem

- Now redefine $a_{it}=A_{it}/P_t$. Using Fisher equation, we have

$$
\max_{\{c_{it}\}} \mathbb{E}_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_i^t\left(u(c_{it})-v(N_t)\right)
$$

subject to

$$
c_{it}+a_{it}\le (1+r_t^p)a_{it-1}+Z_t e_{it}
$$

$$
a_{it}\ge 0
$$

and

$$
r_{t+1}^p=r_t,\quad t\ge 0
$$

$$
1+r_0^p=(1+r_{ss})\frac{P_{ss}}{P_0}
=
(1+r_{ss})\frac{1+\pi_{ss}}{1+\pi_0}
$$

- Same for gov budget constraint, fiscal rule continues to keep $(1+r_t)B_t$ constant
- Even with $r_t$ rule, inflation now matters for aggregate demand due to nominal revaluation! Use Phillips curve:

$$
\pi_t=\kappa_w(Y_t-1)+\beta\pi_{t+1}
$$

## Slide 20: Output effect of a monetary policy shock

- Fisher effect: inflation redistributes from bondholders to taxpayers
- Those agents have higher MPCs, this boosts demand (effect bigger with steeper P.C.)

## Slide 21: The stock market

The stock market

## Slide 22: Introducing a stock market

- Calibration with $A_{ss}=B_{ss}=Y_{ss}$ not realistic
  - Households hold a lot more wealth than this, $\sim 5$ GDP in the U.S.
  - Invested (at least) in stocks in **addition to** bonds
- To incorporate this, change calibration to have markups $\mu>1$, $A_{ss}/Y_{ss}=5$
- Firm problem now implies

$$
\frac{W_t}{P_t}=\frac{1}{\mu}
$$

and dividends

$$
d_t=(1-\tau_t)\left(1-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)Y_t
$$

- Stocks are claim to dividends with price $p_t$
- A mutual fund owns bonds and stocks, sells shares to hh’s with return $r_t^p$

[Households owning stocks and bonds directly similar, see portfolio lecture]

## Slide 23: Asset pricing conditions

- No arbitrage condition for the mutual fund:

$$
1+r_t=\frac{p_{t+1}+d_{t+1}}{p_t},\quad t\ge 0
$$

- Ex-post return to mutual fund holders between $t$ and $t+1$:

$$
r_{t+1}^p=r_t
$$

- What is the ex-post return between $t=-1$ and $t=0$?
- Stock market price at date 0 is

$$
p_0=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\left(\prod_{u=0}^{s-1}\frac{1}{1+r_u}\right)d_s
$$

In steady state, just

$$
p_{ss}=\frac{d_{ss}}{r}
$$

- Total ex-post return is

$$
1+r_0^p=(1+r_{ss})\omega+\frac{p_0+d_0}{p_{ss}}(1-\omega)
$$

where $\omega$ is the s.s. share of bonds in mutual funds portfolio, $B_{ss}/A_{ss}$.

## Slide 24: DAG of model with traded markups

- Goods market clearing:

$$
Y_t=\mathcal{C}_t\left(r_0^p,\{r_s\},\{(Y_s-T_s)/\mu\}\right)+G_{ss}
$$

Image note: The DAG has a box labeled “exogenous: $r$; unknown: $Y$.” This box sends $r$ to Fiscal, sends $Y$ to “Post-tax inc. & div.,” sends $r$ to Asset pricing, and sends $Y$ directly to the goods market. Fiscal sends taxes $T$ to Post-tax income and dividends. Post-tax income and dividends sends $Z$ to HH and dividends $d$ to Asset pricing. Asset pricing sends $r_0^p,r$ to HH. HH sends consumption $C$ to the goods market.

## Slide 25: Impulse response to AR(1) monetary shock

- Calibration: $B_{ss}/Y_{ss}=1$

$$
dY=M^rdr-\frac{M}{\mu}B_{ss}dr+\frac{M}{\mu}dY+mA_{ss}dr_0^p
$$

Term labels:
- $M^rdr$: Direct interest rate effect
- $-\frac{M}{\mu}B_{ss}dr$: Tax effect
- $\frac{M}{\mu}dY$: Labor income effect
- $mA_{ss}dr_0^p$: Cap. gain effect

New capital gain effect!

Magnitude governed by $m$, the MPC out of capital gains (low in data & model, here about 1 cent on the dollar per quarter).

## Slide 28: Neutrality result

- No! For the calibration with $B_{ss}=0$ and $u(c)=\log c$, we prove (see ARE):

$$
dY=-C\,U\,dr
$$

where

$$
U\equiv
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots \\
0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots \\
0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & \cdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots
\end{pmatrix}
$$

- Monetary policy effects in HANK and RANK are the same for any $dr$!
- Werning (2015) result version 2 (log utility + “acyclical liquidity”)
